Round 5 / handcrafted miniature exploration

G: a tactile world, not just a softer face

Your preferred original chapter 34 remains the leading reference. G is a contrasting experiment based on original illustration 34 in chapter 1: a plush-like PinPin with a broad cream muzzle, small dark eyes, short fabric paws and a handmade setting. Illustration numbers and chapter numbers are different.

What G is actually testing

Small dark eyes, fabric-like cheeks and rounded paws replace F's more animated face. Woven cloth and miniature props extend the change into the environment. The target is a photographed handmade-set appearance, not a claim that these AI-generated scenes are photographs of physical puppets.

Existing F sheets provide event and framing references; the original image supplies the material direction. F and G keep the same panel counts, but bodies, expressions, props and some framing also change. This is a comparison of complete treatments, not a single-variable experiment.

My assessment after all five chapters

Homecoming G is the clearest character match to the plush reference. Garden G carries the materials into some plants. Morning G improves the serving action but still needs distinct family identities. Flight G exposes the limits of restrained faces and tight framing. Night Shift G changes the overall style least: many surfaces still resemble F.

I would retain original chapter 34 as the primary direction. G offers a useful alternative for intimacy, but it has not yet established a consistent handmade world or an equally expressive ensemble.

Historical families / possible directions

What is already in the repositories?

These seven overlapping families organize the artwork reviewed so far. The original chapter 34 treatment is now separated explicitly, following your preference. They are not seven official characters or an exhaustive count of variations. Body shape, medium and costume can be combined. Each trial below names what it borrows and what it changes.

Soft dimensional storybook / original chapter 34

Observed: Detailed fine quills, small hedgehogs in substantial wooden rooms, soft rounded volume, textured cloth and light that travels through the scene.

Preferred direction: Miguel's preferred historical direction. Preserve the dimensional surfaces, natural-looking bodies, room scale and warm/cool light relationships rather than flattening them.

Boundary: A new rendering can drift into oversized animation eyes or glossy toy surfaces. A warm color filter alone does not reproduce these originals.

F now tests this direction in chapters 3, 24, 28, 32 and 34. F is an unapproved interpretation of a preferred historical reference.

Tactile miniature / plush-like

Observed: Stubby paws, small dark eyes, broad soft muzzle, tactile fur and the sense of a tiny physical object.

Possible direction: G now tests the tactile finish itself: a fabric-like character in a handmade miniature world. Earlier D borrowed compactness while translating the surface into flatter paint.

Boundary: A handmade-set appearance is present historically and is deliberately explored in G. Soft fur alone is insufficient: the environment must also read as crafted, and simple faces must still support clear acting.

Flight D borrows compactness, not the photographed surface. G explores the material direction in chapters 3, 24, 28, 32 and 34.

Animal-shaped / naturalistic

Observed: Pointed muzzle, low quilled oval, small eyes and four short limbs; the flight image makes the horizontal body especially clear.

Possible direction: An animal-first PinPin, using low resting and flying poses with selective upright gestures.

Boundary: Object handling must remain anatomically convincing. Flat rendering is a new translation of these dimensional references.

C now tests this family in chapters 3, 28, 32 and 34. E used C as its anatomical control in round 3; the new C/F comparison also changes framing and dimensional treatment.

Loose hand-drawn / expressive color

Observed: Loose colored strokes and simplified forms. Image 55 is a gentle mother-and-child drawing; image 74 is a much more saturated, energetic painting. They are related possibilities, not identical techniques.

Possible direction: Either an intimate crayon treatment or a bolder, color-led painted book.

Boundary: A trial of soft crayon does not test the full expressive-color range of this family.

Homecoming A tests the softer end. E now tests the saturated, brush-led branch in chapters 3, 32 and 34, combined with C's animal-shaped anatomy.

Rounded expressive storybook

Observed: Rounded belly, large dark eyes, tiny paws and clearly readable emotion. The originals often use detailed, dimensional surfaces.

Possible direction: Preserve the rounded anatomy and expressive face while simplifying the surface into flat shapes.

Boundary: Too little muzzle or too much eye can make him feel like a generic mascot. The exact flatter finish is proposed, not recovered from every reference.

Flight D combines this face language with miniature-like compactness.

Articulated upright storybook figure

Observed: Projecting muzzle, more visible arms and legs, clothes, and deliberate walking or pointing poses. Bare feet and shoes both occur.

Possible direction: A more active, upright PinPin with readable stepping and handling poses.

Boundary: The body can become too human-shaped. Coats and boots are separate choices, not mandatory anatomy.

Homecoming B combines this anatomy with comic contours; it is not a direct copy of image 75.

Outlined comic / sequential storytelling

Observed: Strong contours, readable faces and props, multiple sequential moments. The farm collection itself mixes rendering treatments.

Possible direction: A flatter, clearly outlined comic using whichever body family we choose.

Boundary: Comic presentation does not require the green costume or human-like legs. Those choices should not be bundled by accident.

Homecoming B borrows the contour language. The flatter Flight D is a new synthesis, not an exact farm-page match.

Green forest wizard: costume, not a separate anatomy

Original images 32 and 40 and farm pages 01, 08, 14 and 15 establish the leaf hat and green cloak. That outfit can sit over different bodies. It is not another painting style, and it is not automatically appropriate for an earlier passage.

Earlier rounds explored flatter drawing. F now deliberately tests the dimensional finish you prefer in original chapter 34. It borrows both rendering and anatomy, not just expression; that change in direction does not change the flat, scroll-first report layout.

Chapter trial / six directions

Chapter 3: Home, Sweet Home

Why this chapter tests the design

It was selected randomly from chapters 2-11. It is short enough to compare its whole narrative: homecoming, supper, bath, drying and bedtime.

The opening explicitly says его маленькие сапожки (his little boots); Mother then cleans them. Removing boots from this scene would contradict the text. Adding long human-like legs is not required by it.

Bathing and bedtime remove the costume. The design must still read as the same child through his face, back, paws, scale and relationship with his mother.

What stays fixed in the trials

The same five beats, child on the left, mother on the right, same domestic props, no wizard outfit, and boots at the doorway only. The full eleven source paragraphs and an English reading translation follow the visual comparison.

A, B and C are bundled directions: anatomy and drawing treatment both change. E is a more focused painting-treatment comparison against C, although generation can still cause anatomy or expression drift. This is an exploratory comparison, not a single-variable experiment. Five illustrated panels are not a new book-pagination decision.

What the homecoming treatments teach us

B: The visible legs and clearer boot shape make the action legible, but make him look taller and more self-assured.

C: C's pointed face and low seated pose feel less like a human child. The clearer two-boot inventory improves continuity, but does not by itself settle foot anatomy.

2. Carrots and conversation

A: The mother-to-child size contrast carries tenderness. Broad quill strokes preserve the loose drawing reference.

3. The herbal bath

A: The rounded face remains recognizable without boots or clothes. The quills form an expressive outline.

B: The more controlled muzzle, ears and paw shapes stay legible against water and herbs.

C: C's profile and quill mass distinguish him even with the lower body hidden. Smaller eyes still communicate attention, although less emphatically than B's.

All three retain a nonhuman hedgehog body and need no wizard accessories to identify the child. This does not yet prove continuity with the later farm version.

4. Drying by the fire

A: The towel and closed eyes give the clearest feeling of physical comfort. The warm orange palette becomes too uniform.

B: The face, towel and mother's paw are more clearly separated. The child still has distinct bare feet.

C: C curls low beneath the towel; this is the clearest contrast with B's seated upright figure. His mother dries the quills rather than simply presenting a towel.

This is the useful identity test: can we recognize him with nearly all of his silhouette covered? Both retain the muzzle, ears and quill line.

5. Bedtime

A: The mother's lean and the child's sleeping expression communicate the kiss without dialogue bubbles.

B: A more defined moss-and-leaf bed and stronger contour make the last action especially clear.

C: C makes a compact curled sleeping shape, with the mother's muzzle close to his forehead. Its intimacy shows that animal-like anatomy need not lose warmth.

All three add a quilt and use closely related staging. These are reasonable adaptation choices, not evidence that either visual style is uniquely correct.

My reading: A is stronger in loose-drawn tenderness; B is stronger in action clarity; C makes the animal-shaped alternative credible in domestic scenes. That is an editorial judgment about these outputs, not proof that every illustration in those families behaves this way. These homecoming sheets do not test running, complicated object handling, the later wizard outfit, or how PinPin and PomPom should differ.

C and D: what changes when the character flies?

Takeoff
C uses a horizontal animal body with dangling paws; D keeps a compact upright oval. Both show the paw connection and both companions off the ground. C's paw overlap is less clear; D's small round hands are easier to read.
Expression
C conveys quieter wonder through posture and muzzle direction. D's large eyes and open mouth make excitement immediate, but reduce the difference between astonishment and the final contemplative moment.
Candle
Both show a recognizable wax candle in panels 3-5. D also adds a flame-like glow above Lilia's paw in panel 1; it is not needed by the passage and should not become an extra recurring prop. C has a smaller spell-like light at takeoff too.
Scale and cave
Both widen the view during ascent and return to a closer conversation at the entrance. The small figures in panel 4 are appropriate for landscape scale, but their expressions cannot be judged there at phone size.
Rendering
D comes closer to flat painted shapes. C still contains a lot of fur shading and dimensional lighting. C is a useful anatomy trial, not yet a successful strict-flat finish.
Cross-chapter identity
Homecoming C and Flight C share the pointed muzzle and quill mass, but the flight version has slightly larger, more expressive eyes. Repeating a direction has not yet locked a production model. Neither flight sheet establishes a consistently identifiable single golden spine.

The useful comparison is no longer only crayon versus ink. We can now ask whether PinPin should move like a little woodland animal or like a rounded upright storybook character. Both have historical support; neither trial is approved.

The imagined marriage wish is retained in the source text, but no wedding is invented in the illustrations. The cousin's claim about PinPin being out of bed all night remains a character's statement, not an independently established timeline.

Historical evidence / the wider review

What changed my mind

The useful distinctions are body shape (low animal, compact upright, articulated upright), rendering (plush-like, naturalistic, painterly, dimensional, inked), costume and character identity. They overlap. Counting them as four or six exclusive character versions would give a false sense of precision.

The following six evidence groups are arguments, not a claim that there are exactly six variants.

Evidence group 1

Softness is not one fixed anatomy

The early pictures include both a plush miniature and freely drawn shapes. The common strength is intimacy, not a single eye size, muzzle length, or technique.

Evidence group 2

Animal anatomy remains a real alternative

Naturalistic pictures should not be dismissed as incapable of emotion. They express through posture, proximity and environment, while giving less room to exaggerated facial acting.

Evidence group 3

The strongest counterexample is inside chapter 16

These are three pictures associated with the same snowy journey, not a child-to-adult timeline. Clothing, hands, feet and proportions change together. My earlier claim that one compact body was the established answer was too strong.

Evidence group 4

Rendering and costume are separate decisions

Calling everything 'storybook' hides important differences. A painterly animal, a dressed dimensional character and a flatter comic figure can share a story without sharing a design system.

Evidence group 5

The wizard is recognizable, but continuity is not solved

The later green outfit is a strong identity cue. It must not conceal a different face or body each time. The original's family groups also require care about which hedgehog is the protagonist.

Evidence group 6

Some apparent variants are not PinPin evidence

Subject identification comes before character design. A mentor, a bird or an uncertain group cannot silently be counted as another version of the hero.

The existing farm storyboard

What survives across six very different moments

The leaf hat, green coat, pale muzzle and small size beside the bear are more stable than the rendering. The pages move between flatter ink drawing and soft dimensional paint. They are valuable references, but not a perfectly consistent model sheet.

Revised recommendation / conditional, not canonical

A clear identity, with room to act.

The leading reference is now your preferred original chapter 34, rather than my earlier preference for a compact upright model. A and B remain useful alternatives, but C shows that an animal-shaped body can also carry tenderness. Flight D demonstrates a flatter rounded alternative. Supported by sources
A dark nose, quilled back, small scale, expressive interaction and, later, a green leaf-hatted wizard identity. Boots have explicit support in chapter 3, original image 75 and the farm pages. There is no evidence-based reason to prohibit them.
My design choice
Keep the muzzle recognizable but not extremely long; no visible neck; short limbs that can actually hold a carrot and walk. Preserve the child-to-mother relationship through consistent relative size. These are choices for continuity, not recovered immutable rules.
Footwear rule
Two tiny boots at arrival; remove them before supper and the bath. Never add a long shin merely to fit a boot. A neutral barefoot reference is useful for checking anatomy, not a ban on narrative footwear.
Limb-count rule
Exactly four limbs: two forelimbs and two hind limbs. Hands are the front paws, never an extra pair. Painting rule

Costume rule
Do not import the farm outfit into this early domestic chapter. The final chapter explicitly introduces magical gifts, but earlier green-clothed artwork exists too. A continuous costume timeline must be checked against each passage, not inferred from color alone.
Golden spine
Chapter 2 says the golden spine has been with him since birth. It should be a single identifiable hedgehog spine if adopted as a visual marker, not a feather or a fully yellow back. None of the twenty displayed trial sheets has established its consistent placement. E's yellow quill strokes make the distinction between a golden spine and general highlights even less reliable.

Exact eye shape, muzzle length, quill silhouette, how much visible leg is acceptable, and how the brothers differ. These sources and twenty trial sheets do not establish a definitive PomPom model.
